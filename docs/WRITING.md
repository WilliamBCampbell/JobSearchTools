# Writing standards

Write for a person deciding whether the candidate can do the job. Use concrete evidence and natural language. A polished claim is still wrong if its source does not support it.

## Resumes

Match the requirements to evidence across the whole career. Keep formal job titles and dates accurate; state team-lead responsibility in the description when it was not the title. Keep each accomplishment under the employer/project where it happened. Avoid a generic duty list or copying the last resume's bullets.

Lead each employer with the most relevant supported work. Compare alternatives and record why the selected examples win. Cover required skills honestly, then use available space for complementary technical depth, customer delivery, leadership or outcomes. Do not stop with half a page when stronger evidence remains. Do not shrink type or add filler just to fill a page; experienced candidates can use two pages when useful.

Use separate bullets for separate accomplishments. Explain the problem, candidate action and actual outcome when known, without forcing every bullet into the same formula. Include relevant exact job terms only when they mean the same thing as the candidate's experience. Do not call keyword overlap an ATS score.

Include confirmed professional contact information and portfolio links. Use readable type, ordinary headings and a layout that extracts in sensible order. Verify the PDF text and every rendered page. A visually attractive PDF can still have bad text order.

## Cover letters

Normally use three or four short paragraphs, about 250-400 words on one page. Employer limits take precedence. Check whether the form asks for a letter before spending time on one.

Open conversationally with what drew the candidate to this role and a specific, sourced reason for the match. Do not insert a standalone job-board sentence merely to satisfy a template. Use a verified hiring-manager name if available; never invent a referral or personal connection.

Tell one or two relevant problem-solving stories rather than listing the resume. Put relevant technical tools near the proof when useful. Explain the desired transition or location/travel context where it helps. Close with a concrete invitation to discuss the work and an appropriate portfolio link. Do not make every paragraph a list of traits.

## Application answers and interview sheets

Answer every part of the actual question within its limit. Use confirmed facts and preserve uncertainty. A request for a GitHub URL plus project contributions needs both. Do not invent survey choices, product usage or qualifications. If the employer requires unaided answers, the candidate writes those answers and the agent handles permitted administrative work.

Interview notes should sound speakable. Show the business problem, personal contribution and result. Label proposed approaches as future actions. Product facts need context: what customer problem do they address, and why does that matter to this role? Keep commercial trivia out unless it helps explain a decision.

## Final self-check

- Read the text aloud. Would the candidate say this to a colleague?
- Remove filler, dramatic claims, generic enthusiasm, em dashes and repetitive paragraph patterns.
- Replace vague verbs such as leverage, utilize and streamline with the actual action.
- Verify every number, title, date, employer claim and level of ownership.
- Name the process instead of saying “those workflows” without an antecedent.
- Remove unnecessary internal table names and confidential details.
- Separate personal, team and hypothetical actions.
- Check source limits and the claim map. Do not let prose make a stronger claim than the evidence.

Original candidate writing can guide rhythm and vocabulary. It cannot justify inventing new facts or mimicking an unrelated person's voice.
